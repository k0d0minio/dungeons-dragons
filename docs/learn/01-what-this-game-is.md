# What this game actually is

Somebody at the table — Jamie — describes a place. You say what your character does about
it. Sometimes the answer is obvious and you just do it. Sometimes it could go either way,
so you pick up a die and let it decide. That loop is the whole game. Everything below is
detail hung off it.

There is no board, no timer and nothing to install. What there is: a person running the
world, four to six people playing one character each, and a handful of dice on the table
between you.

## The three people in the room

**You are one character.** One person, invented by you, with a name and a temper and a
reason for being here. You speak for them and only them. Nobody else gets to say what
your character does — not even the DM.

**Jamie is the DM.** He plays the world: the innkeeper, the wolves, the weather, the
locked door. He describes what you can see and decides what happens after you act. He is
not trying to beat you. A DM who wanted to kill your character could do it in one
sentence; the job is to make the evening a good story, and that needs you alive and in
trouble.

**Everyone else is a character too.** You are on the same side. The interesting decisions
are usually the ones you argue about as a group.

## How a moment plays out

1. Jamie describes the situation. "The door is barred from the other side. You can hear something breathing behind it."
2. You say what you want to do, in plain words. Not "I make a Strength check" — _"I put my shoulder into the door."_
3. If it can obviously work, it works. If it obviously can't, it doesn't. If it's a real question, Jamie asks for a roll.
4. The roll answers it, and Jamie describes what happens next.

Step 2 is the one beginners skip. Say the thing your character does; the rules name for it
is Jamie's problem, not yours.

## Your character is a small pile of numbers

Everything about your character eventually turns into a number the dice get compared
against. You have six [[ability-score|ability scores]] — how strong, how quick, how tough,
how clever, how perceptive, how charming — and each one gives you a
[[modifier]] you add to rolls that depend on it.

Your [[class]] is what you do — fight, sneak, cast spells, heal. Your [[species]] is what
you are, and your [[background]] is who you were before the adventure started. Those three
choices hand you almost everything else on your sheet.

The rest is bookkeeping: how much damage you can take before you drop
([[hit-points|hit points]]), how hard you are to hit ([[armour-class|Armour Class]]), and a
list of the things you are good at.

> You don't have to hold any of this in your head. It's all printed on your sheet, and the
> sheet is on your phone.

## Nobody at this table is an expert

The rules are long. Nobody knows all of them, including Jamie, including people who have
played for twenty years. What happens in practice is that somebody makes a ruling, the
game keeps moving, and it gets looked up later if it mattered.

So the bar for playing well is low and it is not "knows the rules". It is: pay attention,
say what your character does, and don't spend three minutes deciding while five people
wait.
