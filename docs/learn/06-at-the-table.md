# How a session works

A session is one evening — three or four hours, snacks, a table, everyone's phone out with
their sheet on it. Here's the shape of one, so the first one isn't a surprise.

## What to bring

A set of dice, if you have one. Somebody will have spares if you don't; you need a d20 for
the big rolls and a handful of smaller dice for damage. This app deliberately doesn't roll
for you — picking up a die and watching it land in front of everyone is most of why people
sit at a physical table at all.

Your phone with your sheet open, something to drink, and a pencil if you like writing
things down. Nothing else.

## The evening in four parts

**Getting going.** Ten minutes of catching up and reminding each other what happened last
time. If it's session one, this is where everybody says who their character is, out loud,
in a couple of sentences.

**Exploring and talking.** The bulk of it. Jamie describes places and people; you say what
you do, poke at things, ask questions, argue about the plan. No turn order — it's a
conversation. Rolls happen when something's genuinely uncertain, and most of this part has
no rolls in it at all.

**Fights and other clocks.** When seconds start to matter, the table switches into
[[initiative|turn order]] and stays there until it's resolved. See **Your turn** — this is
the part with rules in it, and it's maybe a quarter of an evening.

**Resting and wrapping up.** Somewhere in there the party stops. A
[[short-rest|short rest]] is about an hour: spend some [[hit-dice|hit dice]] to patch
yourself up, and some class abilities come back. A [[long-rest|long rest]] is a night's
sleep: [[hit-points|hit points]] back to full, [[spell-slot|spell slots]] back, half your
[[hit-dice|hit dice]] back, and a caster gets to change which
[[prepared-spell|spells are prepared]]. Then somebody says "shall we stop there" and the
evening ends.

## Turn order, practically

When a fight starts, everyone rolls [[initiative]] — one d20 plus your Dexterity
[[modifier]] — and calls the number out. Jamie writes down the order, highest first, and
that order holds until the fight is over.

A [[round]] is one pass through everybody. It's six seconds of story time, however long it
takes at the table. On your [[turn]] you move, take one [[action]], maybe a
[[bonus-action|bonus action]], and you're done.

The most useful table habit in the game: **decide what you're doing before your turn
arrives.** Six people each taking two minutes is a twelve-minute round, and nobody enjoys
the twelfth minute.

## Talking as your character

You can do voices or not. Both are completely normal and nobody minds.

Two ways to say a thing, and they're equally valid: _"I tell the guard we're with the
merchants"_ works exactly as well as putting on a voice and saying it. Speak in whichever
one you're comfortable with, and switch mid-sentence if you like.

If you want somebody to do something they wouldn't otherwise do, that's usually a check —
Persuasion, Deception, Intimidation. But say the thing first. What your character actually
says changes what Jamie asks for, and sometimes means no roll at all.

## When you don't know a rule

Say so. "I want to do X — is that a thing?" is a completely normal sentence at a D&D table
and you'll hear it from experienced players every session.

Jamie will either know, or make a ruling on the spot so the game keeps moving, and the
correct version can be looked up afterwards if it turns out to matter. Nobody stops the
evening to read a rulebook. The reference chapters in this app are here for exactly the
"afterwards" case, and they work with no signal.

## The unwritten rules, which matter more

**Your character can be wrong; you shouldn't be a nuisance.** Playing someone greedy,
cowardly or rude is great. Wrecking the evening and blaming your character isn't. The
distinction is obvious in practice.

**Nobody speaks for anybody else's character.** You can suggest, argue, plead. You can't
decide.

**Say yes to other people's ideas more than you think you should.** Most memorable
sessions come from somebody's daft plan that the table went along with.

**Pay attention when it isn't your turn.** Half of playing well is just having watched what
happened.

**Losing is part of it.** The plan will fail, someone will drop to zero
[[hit-points|hit points]], the door will be the wrong door. That's the game working, not
the game going wrong.

## For your very first session

You need almost nothing. Know your character's name, roughly what they're good at, and the
five numbers at the foot of **Reading your sheet**. Turn up. Say what you do.

Everything else you'll pick up in the first hour, the same way everybody else at the table
did.
